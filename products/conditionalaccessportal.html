Access control · running today

Your policies, and what they actually let through

Conditional Access is the front door to a Microsoft 365 tenant, and almost nobody can say with confidence what their own policy set permits. ConditionalAccessPortal inventories every policy, checks the coverage you should have, and draws the paths that reach your resources — then confirms a sample of them against Microsoft's own evaluator and tells you where the two disagree.

Ask for a policy review See the price

What it does

  • Inventories every policy — enabled, disabled and report-only, with the targets, grant controls and client-app conditions that decide what each one does. Read through one narrow permission: policy read, and nothing that could enumerate your directory.
  • Checks five baselines — the coverage a tenant should have and usually half has. Each is a named expectation with a rationale, not a score: administrators covered by MFA, legacy authentication blocked, device compliance required, sign-in risk conditioned, and exclusions accounted for.
  • Draws the paths — principals, device classes, policies and resources in four lanes, showing which combinations reach what. It separates a block from a deny: one is a policy you wrote to stop something, the other is a grant the path cannot satisfy. Both end in no access, and sending an administrator to the wrong policy wastes the afternoon.
  • Confirms against Microsoft — a sample of paths is re-run through Entra's own What-If evaluation, and every edge carries how much authority stands behind it.
  • Writes back, once you approve it — enable, disable, or move a policy to report-only. The approval is per change, not per contract.
  • Diffs against a repository — connect a Git repo of policy JSON and see where the live tenant has drifted from what is written down. Read-only throughout.

Why it matters

A Conditional Access estate grows by exception. Someone excludes a service account for a migration that finished two years ago; someone adds a policy in report-only to test it and never enables it; someone writes a rule that a device filter quietly makes unreachable. Nothing breaks, so nothing gets noticed.

The portal in Entra shows you the policies. It does not tell you which of them a given person on a given device actually meets, and it will not tell you that your break-glass account is one policy change away from being locked out along with everyone else.

The five baselines

Each is a named expectation with a reason attached, evaluated against the facts projected out of your policies rather than against raw Microsoft schema — so a Graph change breaks one projection with tests around it, not every tenant's coverage verdict.

  • Administrators require MFA — and authentication strength counts, so a tenant that moved past the built-in control is not reported as having no MFA at all.
  • Legacy authentication is blocked — the protocols that cannot do MFA.
  • Device compliance is required — compliant or hybrid-joined.
  • Sign-in risk is conditioned on — the signal exists; something has to read it.
  • Exclusions are accounted for — the ones added for a migration that ended.

An unrecognised or unreadable policy satisfies nothing: every baseline needs a positive signal, so the failure direction is always toward reporting a gap you may not have, never toward a green tick you have not earned.

The part worth reading

An answer that tells you how much to trust it

Any tool can draw a diagram of your policies. The question that matters is whether the diagram is right, and most tools answer it by not raising the question.

Computed

Our own evaluation, over the whole policy set. Complete, and it is our claim rather than anyone else's. Every edge starts here.

Confirmed

Microsoft's own What-If evaluation agreed, for a real principal we sampled. Higher authority than anything we can assert on our own.

Unconfirmable

We could not sample honestly — nobody sits inside the include set and outside every exclusion, or the policy carries a device filter this build cannot fully parse. An admitted gap, which is worth more than a confident wrong tick.

And a disagreement is the product, not an embarrassment

If Microsoft's evaluator says a policy does not apply where we drew an edge, that is reported to you as a mismatch rather than quietly corrected. A map that silently fixed itself would be hiding the more important fact — that it had been wrong, and probably still is somewhere you did not sample. Our evaluator drifting from Entra's semantics is exactly the thing you would want to hear about first.

Changing a policy is a decision, and it is recorded as one

Write-back is included in the licence, and that is not a shortcut past this platform's rule that anything writing to your tenant is a separate, deliberate choice. Here the deliberate choice is the approval, per change — which is a stronger promise than paying for a tier once and having standing permission thereafter.

  • One method mutates your directory. The permission is checked there, not only on the route, so nothing reaching it from a queued task or a bulk action can slip past the gate. That is a lesson from our own DNS write-back, where the route checks were decorative until the check moved to the thing that actually writes.
  • It fails closed. Everywhere else in this product an unresolvable entitlement degrades toward doing less. Here doing less means not writing to someone else's directory, so an unanswered question refuses rather than proceeds.
  • The write permission is never granted automatically. It is printed separately in the consent catalogue for deliberate approval. Reads keep working with zero write permissions.
  • A refusal is recorded as a refusal. If Entra returns a 403, that is written down as what happened — we never infer consent from a database flag.
  • The break-glass guard refuses outright. A deploy that would leave your designated emergency-access accounts locked out is rejected, not warned about.

What it does not do

Named here rather than discovered later. This product is running and sold; it is also younger than its neighbours, and the gaps are specific.

It does not scan on a schedule

A scan runs when you ask for one. There is no recurring sweep registered by the product today, so "continuous" is a word we use about ShareCare and MailTrust and deliberately not about this one.

Its findings do not leave the product

Coverage gaps do not flow into PosturePortal rollups, and CompliancePortal reads your Conditional Access policies itself rather than taking our verdict. Joining that fan-out later is a small change; un-joining something already aggregated is not, which is why it waited.

Confirmation catches one direction

We check the paths we drew against Microsoft and report where they disagree. A policy Microsoft applies that we drew no edge for — an access path the map does not show — is not yet detected. That is the more alarming half and we are not going to pretend otherwise.

Its trail is its own

Policy changes and deploys are recorded in this product's tables, with the refusal reasons intact. They are not yet in the platform's tamper-evident audit chain the way ShareCare's remediations are.

Objections, answered straight

Entra already shows me my policies.

It does, one at a time. It does not compose them into what a given principal on a given device meets, does not tell you which of your baselines nothing covers, and does not tell you when its own evaluator and a third party's disagree about your tenant.

Why should I trust your evaluation of my access control?

Partly you should not, which is why every edge is labelled. The parts Microsoft confirmed say so. The parts we could not sample say that too, instead of quietly rounding up.

I am not letting a vendor write to my Conditional Access.

Reasonable, and the product works fully without it: the write permission is separate, never auto-granted, and reads need none of it. If you never grant it, you get the inventory, the baselines, the map and the drift report, and every change stays yours to make by hand.

What does it need to read?

Conditional Access policy read, plus group, user and role reads used only to turn object IDs into names and to pick one representative principal for confirmation. Directory-wide read is deliberately not requested — the evaluation matches on the policy document, so a permission that could enumerate your whole directory is reach this product has no use for.
